# Introduction

In their article "Can transgender TV characters help bridge an ideological divide?", Erica L. Rosenthal and Traci K. Gillig investigate how portrayals of transgender individuals in mainstream television can influence public attitudes, particularly across political lines. They focus on a storyline from the USA Network's show Royal Pains, featuring a transgender teen named Anna. The authors conducted a study with 488 viewers, finding that those who watched Anna's episode exhibited more supportive attitudes toward transgender people. Furthermore, exposure to multiple transgender narratives across different shows significantly reduced the correlation between political conservatism and negative attitudes toward transgender individuals. Notably, news coverage, including high-profile stories like Caitlyn Jenner's transition, did not have the same effect. Key concepts in their research include "identification" with characters, both of which can foster empathy and reduce bias. The study suggests that nuanced, fictional portrayals of transgender people can transcend ideological divides more effectively than factual news stories.

# Core Feminist Concepts
**Intersectionality**:
- Coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw, this concept examines how overlapping identities (race, gender, class, sexuality, etc.) shape individual experiences of oppression and privilege.

**The Male Gaze**
- A term from Laura Mulvey describing how visual media often depict women from a heterosexual male perspective, objectifying female bodies and limiting female agency.

**Queer**
- Once a slur, now reclaimed as an umbrella term for non-normative sexual and gender identities. Also used as a theoretical framework to challenge fixed categories of sexuality and gender.

**Racism**
- A system of advantage based on race. Feminist analyses often focus on how racism intersects with sexism and other forms of oppression, especially in representation and access.

**Social Constructionism**
- The idea that many aspects of identity (gender, race, disability, etc.) are not biologically determined but constructed through social processes, norms, and discourse.

**One-Drop Rule**
- A historical racial classification system in the U.S. stating that anyone with even one Black ancestor is considered Black. It reinforces rigid racial boundaries and white supremacy.

**Heteronormativity**
- The assumption that heterosexuality is the norm, shaping institutions, laws, and cultural narratives. It marginalizes LGBTQ identities and relationships.

# Health, Ability, and Normalcy
**Medicalization**
- The process by which human conditions and behaviors are defined and treated as medical issues (e.g., childbirth, menopause, homosexuality). 
- Often critiques how this pathologizes normal variation, especially in women and marginalized groups.

**Disability**:
- More than a medical condition—it's also a social identity. 
- Feminist disability theory looks at how ableism intersects with sexism and challenges how society disables people through exclusion and inaccessibility.

**Hegemonic masculinity**:
- A specific type of culturally-valued masculinity tied to marriage and heterosexuality and patriarchal authority in the family and workplace, and maintains its privileged position through subordinating other less dominant forms of masculinity 
- (i.e., dominance over men of lower socioeconomic classes or gay men). 

**Emphasized femininity**
- a compliance with the normative ideal of femininity, as it is oriented to serving the interests of men (Connell 1987).
 
**Objectification of women** 
- cutting apart their bodies with the camera frame and re-crafting their bodies through digital manipulation in order to create feminized bodies with characteristics that are largely unattainable by the majority of the population.
 
 **Symbolic annihilation** 
- social groups can lack power in society are rendered absent, condemned, or trivialized through mass media representations that simultaneously reinforce dominant ideologies and the privilege of dominant groups. 

 **De jure**:  existing laws and de facto refers to on-the-ground realities. 
 
 **Tokenism:**
 - Queer theorists are often critical of "sanitized" representation—where a queer character is included only if they are "palatable" to a straight audience (e.g., the "gay best friend" who has no romantic life of his own).
 - Representation is seen as a site of power that shapes how we perceive gender, sexuality, race, and identity.
