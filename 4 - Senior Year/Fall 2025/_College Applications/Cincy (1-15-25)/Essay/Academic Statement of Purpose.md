My research pivoted on multiple domains, which integrated concepts from machine learning, cybersecurity, electronics design, and education to advance both the academic space and real-world applications. 

### Project I: Utilizing Machine Learning and Cybersecurity in Networks [1-2]

My first two conference paper publications, a collaboration with professors Dr. M. Ajmal Khan and Dr. Ahmed Oun, sought out to leverage supervised machine learning classifiers to detect malicious network traffic on a network line. I focused on the cybersecurity aspect of machine learning as packets can be captured to create large datasets to train models effectively even in smaller lab settings. From there, the dataset is reduced so that efficient models can be generated with minimal training. Classifiers are able to act as intrusion detection systems as they will raise a flag whenever they detect an abnormal traffic in the line.

Traditional intrusion detection systems, that rely on either signatures or flow rate calculations, lack versatility against novel malicious activity and can themselves be exploited. As attackers have gotten much more sophisticated due to state-sponsorships and how much more lucrative hacking has become, intrusion detection systems need to constantly be updated to keep up with the demand of network security. Not only is this process is not only inefficient, it relies on vendors to be up-to-date on their security measures. Motivated by the necessity for a safe internet, I proposed the machine learning classifier solution as a way to combat inefficiencies caused by normal intrusion detection systems. I invented a system model which uses a machine learning classifier for every potential attack vector. These classifiers can not only differentiate benign traffic from malicious traffic, it is able specify the type of attack with an accuracy of up to 99%. The results of this study were published to two different conferences where I both presented and defended my findings: the 2023 IEEE 14th Annual Ubiquitous Computing, Electronics & Mobile Communication Conference (UEMCON) and the 2024 IEEE 21st Consumer Communications & Networking Conference (CCNC). This was all done within my freshman and sophomore years of college.

 I have presented this work multiple times to demonstrate its importance in the cybersecurity landscape and to help encourage my peers to also participate in undergraduate research. The need for efficient cybersecurity measures for both consumers and industry cannot be underestimated due to the demand in recent years. I pitched the idea to both universities and industry to educate them on the use of machine learning use in the future cybersecurity landscape.

### Project II: Developing a Novel Transistor Sensor and Analog-to-Digital Converter Using Logarithmic Decay [3]

For my sophmore into junior year, this project was ran concurrently with the third project (see below). Rounding out my third conference publication, the following three professors guided me as research advisors on this project: Dr. Firas Hassan, Dr. Enass Hriba, and Dr. Khalid Al-Olimat. Unlike the previous project, I instead focused on the improvement of both sensor technology and analog-to-digital conversion (ADC) electronics. The one goal was to investigate the viability of flash-ADC architecture, a solemnly used variation whose key differentiator is its processing speed. The other was to discover a method on how to increase the dynamic range of an active transistor sensor without significantly increasing its component cost. For most sensors, the speed that the capacitor drains at low-voltage values reduces the range that the sensor can read. With the rise of computer vision and sensor networking, the need for a high speed and high-dynamic range (HDR) sensor has never been more prevalent.

The solution I developed combined both of the goals into one: a sensor and ADC combination circuit that makes flash ADCs viable in modern electronics and significantly improve the dynamic range of sensors. In particular, I propose the logarithmic step control signal, an eight-region analog signal that increments down by a constant voltage such that it simulates a reverse logarithmic signal. As for the ADC, I proposed a multi-level solution that used an smaller ADC to estimate the region where a signal belongs and then a fine ADC for less-significant values. ### Project III: Educational Learning Tool for K-6 Students [4]

Unlike the rest of the projects, the origin of the project is quite different. During my fourth semester, I took a course called Maker Engineering that Dr. Ammar teaches. In the class, teams must make a product utilizing the knowledge they learned about PCB design. Our project, called the Math Quest, is an interactive STEM learning device targeted at children between kindergarten and sixth grade (K-6) to help them teach the fundamentals of mathematics.

When making this product, the team had to stick to a strict maximum per device cost to minimize the burden placed on school systems. To do so, we developed a custom lightweight microcontroller-based user interface that ran on minimal power. We made sure to include both Type-C charging and minimal user inputs so it can be intuitively used by anyone regardless of their age or disability. I helped present the paper at the 2025 First-Year Engineering Experience (FYEE 2025) to various education professionals in both the mathematics and engineering space. We sought to find a replacement for more expensive tablets and Chromebooks for less-advantaged schools while removing the distractions caused by touchscreens. We also collaborated with the Ada Public High School and had interviews with their principal about potential revisions and constraints for the device.
### Project IV: Securing Energy-Harvesting Wireless Sensor Networks

In my final project of undergrad, I developed various combinatorial malicious attacks for testing against energy-harvesting wireless sensor networks to generate a lightweight intrusion detection system using statistics. For this particular project, it was conceived with the hopes of protecting renewable energy solutions to improve their viability and their prevalence in the energy grid. This project is still a work-in-progress at the time of writing, but I plan on submitting to both IEEE SatC 2026 and IEEE MWSCAS 2026 conferences for my preliminary results. Then, over next semester, I plan on working closely with my advisor to publish a journal article. It will wrap up all of the previous work and introduces the intrusion detection system using the statistical models to flag malicious traffic in the wireless network. The hope is to finalize that journal so that I can use it as a basis for my work throughout graduate school and beyond.
