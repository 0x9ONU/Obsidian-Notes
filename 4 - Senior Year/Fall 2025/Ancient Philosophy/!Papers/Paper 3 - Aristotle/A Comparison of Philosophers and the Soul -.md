### *Categories*

In Aristotle’s early lectures, he introduced his disciples to how substances are defined and sorted. For the sake of understanding the soul, first the types of subjects must be defined and their implications must be defined to further bring upon his understanding of the soul. He defines all substances into four different categories based on two binary relationships called “said of a subject” and “present in a subject” (Categories 2 1a20–1b9). The first relationship is used to define when a substance has a subject-predicate relationship to another, while the second relationship defines a relationship that exists dependently or independently. When something is “said of a subject,” it gives an object specificity (Aristotle) and becomes general when it is absent (man). When something is “present in a subject”, it defines a characteristic that cannot exist without another substance. For example, the color of a dog cannot exist without the dog existing. 

From there, Aristotle further defines the difference between a primary substance and a secondary substance. Both category is a subcategory of a specific substance that creates a hierarchy between a specific individual (Aristole) and a group of specific individuals (Men as a species). Aristotle makes it clear that primary substances are special as they contain the “this” (*tode ti*) of a substance and are the purest form of a substance (Categories 5 3b10-25). Other qualities include not having any contrary and does not admit of any degree. That means that they cannot be contrast (such as the qualities of hot or cold) and ensures that a primary substance is the same as a secondary substance other than it being more specific. 
### *On the Soul*

With this knowledge of substances, the next crucial to go into depth about his theory on both the mind and psychology in his *on the soul* lectures excerpts. In the second book of his lectures, he defines three types of substances. The first type, which is called the Form or *tode ti*, is an actuality that defines a substance’s specificity. The second type, called Matter, is what a substance is made out of at a general level, which acts as a potentiality since it can become a specific kind of thing. A composite substance is a combination of both the forma and matter to create a single individual substance. 

Aristotle, to help define what a soul is, draws the connection between how natural bodies are substances. Natural bodies can either be composed of a body and a life or just a body. Since life is given, the soul of human is what differentiates a living being and a corpse. Therefore, the soul must be the form, with the physical body being the matter as it has the potentiality of life. This would follow that Aristotle believes the soul is the actuality of the natural body. To prevent confusion, he also specifies two types of actuality: of having knowledge, and of excercising knowledge. He would further specify that the soul’s actuality is of knowledge rather than exercising it because a living thing will have a soul regardless of the state it is in. For example, a living thing will not cease to have a soul because it went to sleep. The implication of this is that the soul is the first actuality of the natural body which has what he calls *organs* or instruments. He likens the soul and the natural body like that to an axe: the essence of an axe comes from both its sharpness and its shape. If an axe becomes dull, it only becomes an axe in name only and loses the actuality to be an axe. By this analogy, he implies that the soul cannot be separated from the body and is intrinsically linked (On the Soul 2 414a14-27).

Furthermore, Aristotle will provide the the three primary powers of the soul: nutrition, appetite, and thinking. Each one of these powers forms a hierarchy with each power higher containing the potentiality to contain the previous. For instance, the appetitive power can exist without the power of thinking while also having the potentiality to also have the power of nutrition. However, only the power of thinking can have both the potentiality of appetitive and nutrition since often both are necessary to exercise thinking.
