## Exposition

### *Categories*

In Aristotle’s early lectures, he introduced his disciples to how substances are defined and sorted. For the sake of understanding the soul, first the types of subjects must be defined and their implications must be defined to further bring upon his understanding of the soul. He defines all substances into four different categories based on two binary relationships called “said of a subject” and “present in a subject” (Categories 2 1a20–1b9). The first relationship is used to define when a substance has a subject-predicate relationship to another, while the second relationship defines a relationship that exists dependently or independently. When something is “said of a subject,” it gives an object specificity (Aristotle) and becomes general when it is absent (man). When something is “present in a subject”, it defines a characteristic that cannot exist without another substance. For example, the color of a dog cannot exist without the dog existing. 

From there, Aristotle further defines the difference between a primary substance and a secondary substance. Both category is a subcategory of a specific substance that creates a hierarchy between a specific individual (Aristole) and a group of specific individuals (Men as a species). Aristotle makes it clear that primary substances are special as they contain the “this” (*tode ti*) of a substance and are the purest form of a substance (Categories 5 3b10-25). Other qualities include not having any contrary and does not admit of any degree. That means that they cannot be contrast (such as the qualities of hot or cold) and ensures that the secondary substance is essence of the primary substance. For example, an individual person cannot be “more man” and a horse cannot be any “less horse,” but can exist as their own substance with its own characteristics.
### *On the Soul*

With this knowledge of substances, the next crucial to go into depth about his theory on both the mind and psychology in his *on the soul* lectures excerpts. In the second book of his lectures, he defines three types of substances. The first type, which is called the Form or *tode ti*, is an actuality that defines a substance’s specificity. The second type, called Matter, is what a substance is made out of at a general level, which acts as a potentiality since it can become a specific kind of thing. A composite substance is a combination of both the forma and matter to create a single individual substance. 

Aristotle, to help define what a soul is, draws the connection between how natural bodies are substances. Natural bodies can either be composed of a body and a life or just a body. Since life is given, the soul of human is what differentiates a living being and a corpse. Therefore, the soul must be the form, with the physical body being the matter as it has the potentiality of life. This would follow that Aristotle believes the soul is the actuality of the natural body. To prevent confusion, he also specifies two types of actuality: of having knowledge, and of exercising knowledge. He would further specify that the soul’s actuality is of knowledge rather than exercising it because a living thing will have a soul regardless of the state it is in. For example, a living thing will not cease to have a soul because it went to sleep. The implication of this is that the soul is the first actuality of the natural body which has what he calls *organs* or instruments. He likens the soul and the natural body like that to an axe: the essence of an axe comes from both its sharpness and its shape. If an axe becomes dull, it only becomes an axe in name only and loses the actuality to be an axe. By this analogy, he implies that the soul cannot be separated from the body and is intrinsically linked (On the Soul 2 414a14-27).

Furthermore, Aristotle will provide the the three primary powers of the soul: nutrition, appetite, and thinking. Each one of these powers forms a hierarchy with each power higher containing the potentiality to contain the previous. For instance, the appetitive power can exist without the power of thinking while also having the potentiality to also have the power of nutrition. However, only the power of thinking can have both the potentiality of appetitive and nutrition since often both are necessary to exercise thinking. One of the main purposes of the appetite power of the soul is its power to sense, which is how the soul is able to receive the forms of objects without their matter. Much like how a ring’s characteristics, such as its shape and texture, can be imprinted on a part of hot wax, the soul can remove the matter from an object and creates a sensible form of the object (On The Soul 2 424a18-22). It is able to do this by using an organ of sensing, such as the human eye. An important distinction in this logic is that an object is only sensed when both the potentiality of an object being sensed and the potentiality of the eye sensing an object are actualized at the same time. For example, the eye might not be looking for a red object, but the red object is there, so it is not sensed. Similarly, the eye might be looking for a red object, but it is out-of-sight can only have the potential to being sensed (On the Soul 2 425b27-426a27). To an extreme extent, senses can be disturbed or destroyed depending on the sensed object. For instance, a bright light might cause blindness and makes it such that (On the Soul 3 429a30-429b6).

In the third book of *On The Soul*, Aristotle defines his interpretation of intellect/mind (*nous*) and how it works in regard to the soul. After the senses of the soul remove the matter of an object, it is stored in the imagination. From there, the soul can further grasp the rational form out of the imagination. By doing this process, the mind is able to receive the true forms of the object through the sensation of the natural world. Unlike the other parts of the soul, it does not contain any *organs* and is made purely for receiving forms. Additionally, unlike sensing, it cannot be disturbed or destroyed regardless of the intensity of the intelligible object. Finally, it is important to distinguish the different types of thinking that Aristotle defines. There exists three variations of thinking. The first form of thinking, called potentiality #1, is the level of intellect that humans are born with. Here, the forms are not yet gathered, which means they cannot be used. In the second form, called potentiality #2 OR actuality #1, is when the forms have been gathered through living and sensing objects, but the soul is yet to properly exercise them. Here, the soul is not thinking, but is able to think if it tries to. The final form, called actuality #2, is when the mind both contains the forms and uses them to contemplate the forms. Only in actuality #2 is the mind being used properly and a person becomes an actual thinker (On The Soul 3 429b6-9). 
## Analysis
### Objection

With Aristotle’s definition of the soul and it’s various parts, he provides a convincing argument for how the soul might exist and how humans can properly grasp knowledge through the forms. However, potential objections to his logic exist even from philosophers that came before him. Plato, who was Aristotle’s mentor and teacher at the Academy, opposes the way that Aristotle defines the soul. In the objection, Plato’s definition of the soul and its properties will be briefly described and how Plato will expose it using a metaphorical example.

According to *Phaedo*, his version of the Forms can exclude each other with each thing only being able to partake in one Form at a time. Following this logic, he argues that a soul’s defining function is to give life to a body. Since an object cannot partake in the opposite Form at the same time, Plato concludes that the soul, as the bringer of life, can never succumb to death. Since something that can never die is indestructible, the soul does not perish as it retreats or goes away when its host body perishes. His argument shows that soul is an immortal substance that is able to be separated from the body. He further believes, according to *Meno*, that each soul has the ability to recollect (*Anamenesis*) since every human soul has the knowledge of everything due to its immortality.

Plato, in his fourth book of *Republic*, presents a theory of the soul that opposes Aristotle’s core tenets. Instead of having powers, Plato clearly identifies three main parts of the soul: the rational, spirited, and appetitive (436a-442a). These three parts of the soul generate internal strife as all three parts are in constant conflict with each other. For the healthiest soul, the rational part, with help of the spirited part, rules over the appetitive part to create harmony with moderation in the soul (441e-442b).

As an example, lets delve into the relationship between a piano craftsman and the piano he creates. The craftsman, preexisting the piano, understands the knowledge, skill, and design of a piano independently of seeing a piano. He creates a piano using the various tools and materials around him. Afterwards, he can express his musical skill through the instrument, which gives it life much like that of a soul. If a piano is left to the wayside and must be destroyed, the piano maker can either build or play another piano. He exists separately and before any instrument he chooses to make or play.

Plato would believe that defining a soul as dependent on the body and perishable, like Aristotle, is a fundamental error.  Aristotle fails to account for how humans can recollect knowledge. Much like a craftsman of instruments, a human needs a soul to preexist in order to know how to be “played” such that it can be given life. 

### Response

Aristotle would argue that his definition of the soul is still accurate and that he can correctly explain the knowledge gap that Plato brings up. 

As the counter example, lets go back to the example regarding the pianos. From Aristotle’s perspective, he would make it evident that the soul is not the craftsman, but is rather the playability of a specific piano. The precise materials, string pressures, defects, and hallow body all give it the ability to produce music. The musicality of the piano does not exist before the piano is created, and it cannot survive a destructive blow. If the piano is smashed, it cannot be played. It was the actualization of the piano’s potential, and is therefore dependent on the existence of a piano in the first place.
