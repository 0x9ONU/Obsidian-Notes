## Abstract

## Exposition

Parmenides’ view on science can be deciphered by using six Fragments: two, three, six, seven, eight, and twenty. In his second Fragment, He describes the critical sense of “is” that the rest of his arguments rely on. According to the second passage, he introduces a dichotomy of “what-is” and “what-is-not”. The path of “it is" is attended entirely on the prospects of Truth since “it is not possible for it not be” anything otherwise. The other path; however, is devoid of any Truth and should never be learned, investigate, or declared with good intentions (Fragment 11.2.3-8). Using the abstractness of the second Fragment alone, it opens up two primary questions: what is he referring to when he says “what-is” and what does it mean to be “what-is?” The third and sixth Fragment fills in the details. He declares the subject of “what-is” to be an object that is able to be both thought of and spoken (Fragment 11.6.1). In particular, he specifies that that "what-is" must exists since both thinking and being are one in the same (Fragment 11.3). He also makes it clear that nothing cannot be “what-is” since it cannot exist itself (Fragment 11.6.2-3). In his seventh Fragment, he further defines the importance of understanding the difference between “what-is” and “what-is-not” by illustrating the clash of appearance and reality. As humans see the world, they rely on their perception (such as seeing, hearing, and listening) to make sense of it. Parmenides sees that perception is flawed as people can perceive the world through a combination of Truths (“what-is”) and mental illusions (“what-is-not”) (Fragment 11.7.2-4). He even goes as far to say that our “tongue” is also accursed to deceptive speech and is plagued with falsities (Fragment 11.7.4-5). He instead urges that grasping the world through the means of logic is the only way to obtain a reality such that only the ultimately true “what-is” is left (Fragment 11.7.5-6). He goes as far to say that reality must be grounded in such that existence implies a singular and unmoving Truth (Fragment 11.20). The eight Fragment further clarifies the idea of an unmoving Truth. In particular, he asserts that reality cannot change since the implication of change or motion means that an object in existence would have to undergo a transition. However, this motion of transition would further insinuate the change of something that exists to nothing back into another object, which would be in direct contradiction of Fragment 6 (Fragment 11.8.7-22).  Due to this falsehood of motion, he poises that humans are often too quick to use their senses to categorize their surroundings into opposites. For example, he suggests how fire is often given the characteristic of light in contradiction to the darkness of night. Darkness, from a mortal’s perspective, can be given the quality of “anti-light” as it is without any flame to provide light (Fragment 11.8.56-59). However, as established earlier, the implication of Fragment twenty and eight make it clear that Truth must be unmoving and singular, while the dichotomy of opposites implies that an object can be both moving and plural (Fragment 11.8.7-22 & 11.20). From the perspective of science, he states that science is grounded in evidence. Since science is relies on empirical evidence, it must utilize flawed human perception and could never be fully ingrained with Truth (Fragment 11.7). In a way, he would say that science is much like a language: an imperfect medium that mortals use to try and give both the “what-is” and the “what-is-not” meaning (Fragment 11.7.4-5 & 11.8.33-40). Through his expectations of reality and Truth, Parmenides would dismiss science as being the antithesis of knowledge as it is only a way for mortals to understand their world of appearance and contradicts itself through the use of opposites and perceived evidence. 

## Analysis

### Objection

Even though Parmenides’ argument is rather sound, there are particular shortcomings in his argument against science. For the sake of the argument, the objection will focus on the nature of optical illusions. As the name states, optical illusions happen when the human sense of sight is tricked into believing something false in their environment. The most famous example in nature comes from mirages in deserts. Dehydrated travelers in search for water would often hallucinate oases in the distance and find nothing once they got there. This gave the desert a hostility that was thought to be the work of mythos and the gods. However, the science of today explains the phenomena using logic instead. As light passes through various densities and temperatures of air, it has the tendency to refract and reflect on air particles. In the case of mirages, the hot ground refracts the sunlight that beats down on the desert. From the human eye, this refracted sunlight looks as if there is water on the ground. How optical illusions and sight can be explained by science can be a metaphor of how reality can be distilled from the world of appearance using the scientific method. As humankind uncovers more information about reality, they move away from imagining ideas and begin to think properly up to the standards of Parmenides. Science is able, in a sense, to strip away the illusions of the world of appearance and provide the truth hidden underneath “what-is-not.”
