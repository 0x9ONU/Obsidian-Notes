### Definition of Justice

In the fourth book of *Republic*, Plato provide’s his interpretation on justice. However to properly articulate how Plato defines justice, some background information must be provided from some of his earlier works and other parts of *Republic*. In Plato’s *Phaedo*, Socrates, in what is often described as his “swan song,” discusses two philosophical concepts before his death ( 77e-78a). The first concept is based around his description of the soul (*psyche*). Unlike earlier philosophers, Plato uses the soul as a tool to differentiate between living and nonliving things. This is  further categorized into human and non-human souls by its inclusion of the mind (*nous*) and its desire to search for knowledge and truth (66b-d). Unlike previous philosophers, Plato believes in an epistemological model where both the human body and soul are two separate entities that work both with and against each other to find this truth (64a & 66e-67b). As his second point, Plato gives truth a designation as objects of knowledge called Forms. These Forms exist within the soul and can be recollected (*anamnesis*) over someone’s lifetime (65d–66a & 73ff). 

The second book of *Republic* gives insight into possibilities of the common misconceptions around justice. Particularly, most people think that it is part of human nature for people to take unjust actions because they are inherently lucrative (359d). Therefore, to counteract this behavior, a social structure, or contract, must be in place to prevent unjust actions from taking place. For example, some believe that unjust actions are so profitable that laws are made to make sure no one commits unjust deeds to one another (359a). However, Plato disagrees with these statements and will investigate his definition of justice by providing the instance of a city and how it relates to the individual (368-369a) In *Republic* Book III, he further species that the luxurious city best defines justice. The city is broken down into three main categories of people: the rulers, the auxiliaries, and the people (415a-d). The rulers, which are brought up with proper education, are chosen based on their nature and guide the city (399e). The auxiliaries, which are also known as the guardians, are courageous, spirited, and gentle people who protect the city (373d5-e5). And the people follow their own craft based on where their soul takes them.

In the fourth book of *Republic*, Plato will then define the four following cardinal virtues: wisdom, courage, moderation, and justice. The first three virtues are equivocally mapped to the three classes of the cities. A wise city exhibits good judgement based on the knowledge of its rulers (427d–428e). A protected city shows that the auxiliaries are able to use courage to defend their city out of habit and without second thought through education from the rulers(429d-430b). Moderation is critical of the entire city and shows that everyone can control their pleasures and desires (430d–432a). Justice, at the level of the city, is given as the virtue that allows each class to live out their task of wisdom, courage, or moderation without overlap (433b-d & 434a-b). From there, Plato argues that a city’s character can be equated to similar characteristics in an individual, which allows him to further explore this idea at a lower level (435e). Much like how classes have virtues, he believes that an individual’s soul has three different parts: the rational, the spirited, and the appetitive (436a-442a).  Much like the city, the rational part of the soul rules the rest by wisdom. However, it is not without help since the spirited part takes courageous actions based on the dictation of the rational part. Together, these two parts of the souls control the appetitive part to create harmony through moderation (441e-442b). Therefore, justice, in the individualm is the power for each part of the soul to do its own task without each part meddling in the other (443d-444a).
### Form of the Good

Towards the tail-end Book VI of *Republic*, Plato starts to delve into the importance of the Form of the Good. Being higher than just virtues, it is the pinnacle of knowing the knowledge of the Good and what is both just and unjust (504e-505a) A soul’s very nature is to seek the Good as the form of the Good will lead them closer to true wisdom and knowledge of the forms (505e-506a). Because defining the Good is particularly difficult, Plato utilizes the seventh book of *Republic* to lay out three analogies: the Sun, the divided line, and the cave.

The first of these analogies relates the the Sun in the visible realm to the Good in the intelligible realm. In the visible realm, the Sun provides a source of light so that humans can see visible things using their eyes. In a similar sense, the form of the Good provides the truth in the intelligible realm. By using truth, a human’s soul is able to understand and “see” the Forms. Because of this hierarchy, the Good is considered to be both an epistemological and metaphysical principle. Much like how the Sun is the most important object that provides light to the visible things, the Good is superior to all and shines truth onto the Forms so that the soul can recollect (*anamnesis*) the knowledge of the Forms that is already present in one’s soul (507b-509b) Fig 1. illustrates the dichotomy of the visible and intelligible realm below:

The second analogy utilizes a similar breakdown of the world into both the visible and the intelligible realm, but instead uses four major categories: $A_{1}$, $B_{1}$, $A_{2}$, and $B_{2}$. The $B_{2}$ category relates to all objects that are imaginations that imperfectly mirror of real objects in the visible realm. Images, shadows, and reflections fall under this category. The $A_{2}$ category deals with all of the real versions of these objects such as animals, plants, and manufactured things. $B_{1}$ breaks into the intelligible reality and covers all calculations, mathematics, geometry, and drawn figures where a person will use their knowledge to come to an imperfect conclusion from a hypothesis without grasping the principle of the Good. On the other hand, the $A_{1}$ category is dialectical in nature and represents true understanding. Instead of leading to a conclusion, the hypothesis uses a human’s innate ability to comprehend by reason (*noein*). By using the mind (*nous*) to comprehend the soul (*psyche*), a human comprehends the unhypothetical first principle,the Form of the Good. Plato’s third and most famous allegory comes in the form of a short story. Multiple prisoners are tied down in a cave such that they can only see what is in front of them Behind them, a flame burns brightly which casts itself on the wall in front of them. Their own shadows are cast against the wall, which lets them know that they are not alone. Throughout their days, a warden moves various cut outs of animals, plants, and manufactured things in front of the fire, casting a bold shadow against the wall in front of the prisoners. Since the shadows are all they know, they would believe that the shadows are the truth and themselves are real objects. If one of the prisoners would be let free and shown the puppets of the shadows, he would at first be perplexed and would be unable to accept that the shadows are not more true than the world around him. If he were to then be dragged out of the cave, he would realize the hierarchy of the world. He would see the shadows of objects and the reflection of objects on the water. He would then see the objects themselves and their projections in constellations among the stars. And, above all, he would see the Sun that gives life to all things on Earth. If the freed prisoner would go back down into the cave, he would now not be able to see the shadows as well as he once was able to. This would cause the other prisoners to look down upon him since they can recognize the shadows quicker than he can (414a1-587d7). The cave analogy also brings up the importance of one’s education. In this case, education is defined as having one’s soul changed by truth rather than by appearance (518b-d & 519b). Plato also stresses that the ones who are able to “escape the cave” and become properly educated are destined to become rulers. They must educate those who do not see the truth themselves regardless if they want to or not (520b-d). 
### Argument for Why the Just Person is Happier than the Unjust Person

By laying the foundation of justice and the Form of the Good, Plato is able to answer the question he proposed at the beginning of Book I of *Republic*: “Who is happier? The just person or the unjust person.” Throughout the last three books, Plato provides three major arguments in favor of the just person being happier: (1) the constitutions of the soul, (2) the desires of the soul, and (3) false pleasure of the soul. For the sake of scope, the second argument will be focused on. This argument relies on the three parts of the soul and how each one of them desires a specific type of pleasure. The rational part of the soul seeks truth and loves learning and philosophy. The spirited part seeks control, victory, and high repute and loves honor. The appetitive part seeks only temporary pleasures like food, drink, and sex. This part loves profit and is the hardest to control of the three. To decide which soul is more fulfilled, each part of the soul is mapped to either a philosopher, a victory-lover, and a profit lover. Additionally, three different standards of judging a good life are used: (1) experience, (2) reason, and (3) argument. In terms of experience, a profit-lover has only found appetite and will not experience the other desires. An honored person has had a taste of appetite, but chooses to be courageous instead since he gets the most pleasure out of victory. A philosopher, on the other hand, has experienced all three forms of life and chooses to live through the happiest path in his opinion. Furthermore, reason is used only by a philosopher to gain his experience and argument is an instrument that can only be used by a philosopher. Therefore, the philosopher has to be the most truthful and living by the definition of justice is the most fulfilling life (580d–583a).
