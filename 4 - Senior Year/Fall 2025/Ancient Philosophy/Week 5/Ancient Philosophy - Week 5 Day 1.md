# Plato’s *Meno*: A Transitional Dialogue

## Can Virtue be Taught?

The dialogue begins with the question “Can Virtue be Taught”

Socrates *cannot* answer this question because he does not know what **virtue** is.
- Meno is s[[Ancient Philosophy - Week 5 Day 1]]urprised that Socrates does not know the answer
- *Meno* will also discover that he does not know it either
- Throughout the dialogue, Plato uses the terms such as “remember”, “remind”, and “recollect” (see 71d)
- They are closely related to theory of recollection (*Anamnesis* - which means, “calling to mind”)
- Meno cannot recollect or cannot remember because he has no knowledge.

```ad-quote
title: Meno 71d
Socrates: I do not altogether remember, Meno, so that I cannot
tell you now what I thought then. Perhaps he does know; you know
what he used to say, so you remind me of what he said. You tell me
yourself, if you are willing, for surely you share his views. — I do.
Socrates: Let us leave Gorgias out of it, since he is not here. But
Meno, by the gods, what do you yourself say that virtue is? Speak and
do not begrudge us, so that I may have spoken a most unfortunate
untruth when I said that I had never met anyone who knew, if you and
Gorgias are shown to know.
```

## Meno’s Answer to Virtue

Three definitions from Meno:
1. Managing the city well (71e-72a)
2. To be able to rule over men (73d); and
3. To desire beautiful things and have the power to acquire them (77b)

All of them are wrong answer for the very same reason (see 73a, 73d and 78d)
- It is possible that all three actions can be performed “justly” or “unjustly”; thus, Meno continually begs the question
- Meno cannot remember not only what he has learned from Gorgias, but also the point that Socraees is trying to drive home

## Torpedo Fish

Meno’s Reaction at 80a
- Meno is capable of giving fine speeches about virtue in front of a large audience, but he cannot withstand Socrates’s questionings.
- Much like “the broad torpedo fish”, which has a sting that makes you numb

**Meno’s Paradox**: 
- Neither Meno nor Socrates know the nature of Virtue
- But how can one search for knowledge when we do not know what it is?

```ad-quote
title: Meno's Paradox (80e)
A person cannot search either for what he knows or for what he does know. He cannot search for waht he knows - since he knows it, there is no need for search - nor for what he does not know, for he does not know what to look for.
```

## Recollection

To respond, Socrates tells a story based on a story:
- It is that human soul is immortal and that because soul has seen everything it has knowledge of everything and, thus, what we call learning is “recollection”.

```ad-note
1. Scorates does not claim to *know* that this story is true, although he is inclinde to believe it. 
2. The immortality of the soul is not used as a basis to demonstrate that learning is recolleciton; he only uses the story to introduce the topic.
```

Meno then questions Socrates what he means by “learning is a recollection” 
- Socrates points out that it is not possible for him to “teach” him, but is able to show how a person recollects.
## Geometrical Demonstration

At this point, Socrates goes to ask Meno’s slave, who has never learned any geometry, a series of questions. 
- He insists that he is not teaching, but only questioning.

```ad-summary
title: Summary of The Geometrical Demonstration (81e-85d)
Socrates draws a square and divides it into 4 equal sections and points out that there are 2 squares on each side. The question Scorates, then, poses to the slave is this: How many squares do each side has if we double the area of he square to 8? The first response was 4, which is wrong and should be sixteen. Since the size is larger than 2, but smaller than 4, the slave's next guess is that it is 3. Again, the answer is wrong.
```

- At this point, the slave was perplexed and admits that he does not know the answer

**Apologetic Element (84a-d)**:
- The benefit of being *perplexed*
- It gets rid of delusion and pretentiousness to knowledge and no harm is actually done. 
- In fact, a person is now in a **better** position to attain

## Recollection: “Finding knowledge within oneself”

- Socrates, then, divides the square with the area of 16 into two by drawing 4 diagonals of the square with the area of 4
- Socrates insists that he is not teaching anything during the demonstration and that the slave is not learning from him but *recollecting*. But this recollection must happen in a certain order. The slave has only gained an opinion and not knowledge yet.

```ad-important
Recollection is defined as "finding knowledge wihin oneself"
```

## Is Virtue Knowledge?

Afterwards, they go back to the original question: “Can virtue be taught (or recollected)?” (86c)
- Without knowing the definition of virtue, if it is a kind of knowledge, then, it could be taught/recollected
- *Hypothesis*: Virtue is good (Virtue > Good).

*Assumption*: Good is Beneficial (Good > Benefits)
- But if so virtue is beneficial (virtue > benefits), since virtue is good (virtue > good)
- Both the qualities of the body and soul are all beneficial *only with* knowledge
- Everything directed by wisdom makes us happy, but those directed by ignorance will not.

```ad-important
Socrates concludes that virtue, since it is a good, must be a kind of *wisdom* (88c)
```

## Knowledge vs. Correct Opinion

Both are equally useful in guiding us or directing us to the correct action (97a-b)
- Both always succeed
- Knowledge;, however, is more valuable because it can be “tied down” but right opinion tends to “ran away” (97d-98a)
- It is “tied down” when one can give an account of reason why.
