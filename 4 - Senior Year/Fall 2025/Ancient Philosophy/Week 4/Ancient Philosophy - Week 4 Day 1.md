# Euthyphro

## Socratic Dialogues

**Two** major components:
1. The apologetic (defending Socrates)
2. The philosophical element (raising a certain issue)

Other features:
- Socrates is he main character
- Relatively short
- One subject per dialogue (piety, moderation, courage, etc.)
- Socrates asks, “What is X?” question.
- Socrates is used as a mouthpiece for Plato

### *Aporia*

The main interlocutor in the dialogue *fails* to answer the “What is X?” question successfully.
- Ends in an *aporia* (without passages)
- Although we learn something about the subject in question
- Socrates embodies the virtue in question

```ad-example
Socrates is seeking for a general defiition as opposed to an example to try to embody piousness, moderation, virtuousness, etc.
```

The *problem*: Gods disagree and dispute among themselves about the subject in quesiotn.
- If so, the same hting becomes both pious and impious (but that is not possible)

Euthyphro says: "No gods will disagree about what he is doing; that is, whoever has killed anyone unjustly should pay the penalty"

But, Euthyphor is begging the question: The dispute is not whther “unjust” should pay the panlty, but rather why an actino is “unjust" or what makes it “unjust”.

### Another Try

```ad-tip
title: Another potential solutino
The pious is what all the gods love and the impious is what all the gods hate.
```

*Question*: Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?
- **Nature vs. Affection**: Is there a nature of piety that makes the gods love it? Or is piety, by definition, an affection fo the gods such taht whatever happesn to be loved by the gods becomes pious by the act of love?

Euthyphro selects the first option and his response becomes *circular*. Pious is what all the gods love because it is pious. If so, the god-beloved cannot be identical to pious.

### Euthyphro’s Dilemma

1. Is X loved by God because X is (morally) good? OR
2. Is X (morally) good because God loves it?
