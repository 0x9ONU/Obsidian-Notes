[[Security in Energy Harvesting Networks A Survey on Current Solutions and Research Challenges.pdf]]  [1] is a survey paper published by Pietro Tedeschi, Savio Sciancalepore, and Roberto Di Pietro that contains various methods to both attack and defend energy-harvesting wireless sensor networks (EH-WSN). After previewing the nearly 250 papers that were cited in the article, there were 15 papers that most closely matched our research contributions. 

Two of the papers [2], [3] focused on the potential eavesdropping attacks such that a passive attacker can intercept signals between sensor nodes and their base station. To counter the attack, they suggested utilizing a friendly jammer to interrupt the passive eavesdropper or obfuscate the data by using better scheduling techniques. More abundant research done in the area related to DoS attacks, with the most prominent being with jamming attacks [4-13]. Each of these papers focused on physical attacks that added interference to a network by injecting noise to try and decrease the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) to ruin the connection and cause networks to either get out-of-sync or allow nodes to die (especially in networks where they *wireless transfer energy* as well). There were many approaches to counter such attacks such as detection, traffic management, modeling, splitting energy-harvesting into two phases (sleeping and harvesting), channel hopping, tricking the jammers with fake packets, implementing stricter security/energy policies, channel avoiding (Anti-Jamming OIA), game theory, shifting all power costs to the sender instead of the receiver (power-positive networking), or using acknowledgements to ensure proper routing. The one paper relating to replay attacks takes advantage of the lack of sophisticated security protocols in lightweight IoT devices to manipulate and impersonate nodes, much like our burst attacker [14]. They proposed the ShieldScatter protocol that adds backscatter tags to create both forward and backward signatures that ensure that only legitimate nodes are transmitting and that replayed packets are discarded. Finally, the two papers relating to spoofing describe how transmitting either fake data (active) or manipulating packet tags can be used to spoof traffic and cause network degradation [15-16]. They suggest either the use of lightweight cryptography or wave deflection to determine the location of devices after they propagate to ensure they are coming from a legitimate source.

Overall, the survey papers do not mention our lightweight variation of attack detection that uses a passive eavesdropping and time statistics to detect jamming/DoS attacks in an EH-WSN. It is safe to conclude that our paper has a novel contribution to the literature, and a conference paper with the preliminary results is in order.
