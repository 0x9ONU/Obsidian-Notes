# Introduction to Composites

```ad-summary
Composite materials represent a paradigm shift in engineering, enabling solutions for high-stakes problems like atmospheric re-entry and jet engine efficiency. At its simplest, a composite is a material made from two or more distinct constituent materials. While they exist in nature (like wood), engineered composites allow for the precise tailoring of mechanical, thermal, electrical, and magnetic properties to suit specific industrial applications.
```

# The Two-Phase Structure
Most composites consist of two primary parts:
1. **The Dispersed Phase:** 
	- Usually a ceramic or metal, this provides the desired properties like strength or ductility. 
	- It can take the form of particles, short fibers, or continuous fibers.
2. **The Matrix Phase:** 
	- A polymer, ceramic, or metal that surrounds the dispersed phase.
	-  bonds and transfers loads between the dispersed elements
	- protects them from *environmental* damage.

# Fiber-Reinforced Polymer (FRP) Composites
The most widely used category in modern engineering.
* **Materials:** Includes Glass Reinforced Polymers (GRP/Fiberglass) and Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymers (CFRP). These typically use an epoxy (thermosetting polymer) matrix.
* **Anisotropy:** Unidirectional tapes are highly anisotropic—strong along the fiber axis but weak perpendicular to it. This allows engineers to align strength exactly where it is needed (e.g., hoop stress in pressure vessels).
* **Laminates:** By stacking layers (lamina/plies) at various angles (0°, 90°, 45°), engineers create "quasi-isotropic" laminates that exhibit uniform strength in all in-plane directions.

# Manufacturing Methods
* **Wet Layup:** Manual application of resin to fiber layers using rollers/brushes.
* **Pre-preg:** Using fibers already impregnated with partially cured resin, followed by vacuum bagging and oven curing.
* **Filament Winding:** Machines wind resin-impregnated tape around a mandrel (used for cylindrical objects).
* **Injection Moulding:** Often used for short-fiber composites where fiber orientation is arbitrary.

# Mechanical Performance and "Specific" Properties
The true power of composites is revealed when looking at **Specific Strength** and **Specific Stiffness** (property divided by density). 
* CFRP and GRP far outperform traditional metals like steel, titanium, and aluminum on a per-weight basis.
* **The Size Effect:** Thinner fibers are stronger because they contain fewer microscopic defects. Smaller diameters also increase surface area for better load transfer to the matrix.

# Thermal and Environmental Advantages
Beyond strength, composites offer:
* **Internal Damping:** Excellent for dynamic/vibrational loads.
* **Corrosion Resistance:** The polymer matrix shields reinforcement from moisture and chemicals.
* **Thermal Stability:** Very low thermal expansion coefficients, providing dimensional stability across temperature ranges.

# Drawbacks and Challenges
* **Cost:** Significantly higher than traditional metals.
* **Complexity:** Anisotropic failure modes make modeling and prediction difficult.
* **Joining:** Composites cannot be welded; they are typically bonded with adhesives or fastened with specialized mechanical inserts.
* **Brittleness:** Unlike ductile metals, CFRP fails suddenly at very low strains.
* **Temperature Limits:** Polymer matrices break down above 100-200°C.

# Advanced Composites: Ceramic and Metal Matrices
* **Ceramic Matrix Composites (CMCs):** Used for extreme heat (1000°C+). While pure ceramics are brittle, adding fibers allows for "crack bridging," which prevents sudden failure. Used in jet engine turbines and heat shields.
* **Metal Matrix Composites (MMCs):** Often use aluminum or magnesium. In medicine, magnesium-matrix composites are used for biodegradable bone implants where the degradation rate is controlled by ceramic particles.
* **Functional Composites:** Copper-matrix composites with diamond particles act as high-efficiency heat spreaders for electronics.

# Common and Structural Composites
* **Concrete:** A particle-reinforced composite (cement matrix with aggregate). Recent "bendable concrete" adds polymer fibers for ductility.
* **Sandwich Composites:** A lightweight core (honeycomb or foam) sandwiched between stiff skins (aluminum or CFRP). This creates high bending stiffness similar to an I-beam and is used extensively in satellites and aerospace panels.
