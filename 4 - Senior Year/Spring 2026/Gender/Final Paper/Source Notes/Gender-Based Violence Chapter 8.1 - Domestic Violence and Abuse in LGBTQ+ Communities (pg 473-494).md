# Introduction

This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of Domestic Violence and Abuse (DVA) within LGBTQ+ communities, exploring its definitions, prevalence, specific manifestations, and the unique challenges faced by these groups.

# Defining LGBTQ+ Communities and DVA

- **LGBTQ+ Communities**: Refers to a diverse range of individuals who do not conform to heteronormative or cisnormative models of gender and sexuality. This includes people with minority sexual orientations (e.g., lesbian, gay, bisexual) and gender identities (e.g., trans, non-binary, queer).
- **Domestic Violence and Abuse (DVA)**: Broadly defined as any incident or pattern of controlling, coercive, or threatening behavior between intimate partners or family members, regardless of gender or sexuality. It encompasses psychological, physical, sexual, financial, and emotional abuse.

# Theoretical Frameworks and Challenges

- **Heteronormative Bias**: Dominant understandings of DVA often center on a "public story" of male violence against women in heterosexual relationships. This framework can invisibilize LGBTQ+ experiences and lead to myths that DVA in these communities is rare or less severe.
- **Minority Stress**: LGBTQ+ individuals may experience unique internal and external stressors (e.g., internalized homophobia or societal discrimination) that can be linked to the normalization or use of DVA, though these links are considered correlational rather than causal.
- **Power and Control**: Research suggests that patterns of power and control are the defining features of DVA in both heterosexual and same-sex relationships, rather than gender alone.

# Specific Manifestations of Abuse

Beyond the common forms of physical and sexual violence, LGBTQ+ survivors may face unique tactics known as **identity abuse**:

- **Outing or Closeting**: Threatening to reveal or forcing someone to hide their sexual orientation, gender identity, or HIV status.
- **Weaponizing Identity**: Undermining someone's sense of their gender or sexuality (e.g., saying they are not "gay enough").
- **Trans-Specific Abuse**: For trans individuals, abuse may include "deadnaming" (using their birth name), using incorrect pronouns, or disrupting access to gender-affirming medical treatments.

# Prevalence and Impact
- **High Prevalence**: While rates vary by country and study, evidence consistently shows that LGBTQ+ people are at an equal or increased risk of experiencing DVA compared to their heterosexual, cisgender counterparts.
- **Psychological Abuse**: This is often the most prevalent form of abuse reported across all sexual minority groups.
- **Mental Health Consequences**: Victimization is linked to increased anxiety, depression, PTSD, substance misuse, and suicide ideation. Identity abuse further exacerbates these impacts by undermining a survivor's safety and sense of self.

# Barriers to Support

- **Underreporting**: LGBTQ+ survivors are less likely to seek help from formal agencies like the police or DVA services.
- **Barriers**: Reasons for not seeking help include fear of not being believed, fear of homo/bi/transphobia from professionals, and a lack of services designed specifically for LGBTQ+ communities. Survivors often wait longer to seek support and are more likely to turn to informal sources like friends or therapists.
