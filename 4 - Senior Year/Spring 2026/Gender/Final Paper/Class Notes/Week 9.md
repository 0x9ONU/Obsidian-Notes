## Chapter 17 Summary

Examines how social institutions—family, media, medicine, law, and the prison system—perpetuate gendered violence. The author argues that violence against women and marginalized genders is systemic, not isolated, and is reinforced by societal norms that devalue femininity. The chapter draws on Hussein Bulhan’s definition of violence as a structural issue encompassing colonization, poverty, and racism. Evidence includes statistics on intimate partner violence, historical legal injustices like marital rape laws, and barriers marginalized women face in reporting abuse due to systemic racism and immigration fears. The critique of psychological syndromes such as Battered Women’s Syndrome highlights how medical frameworks pathologize survivors rather than addressing root causes. Key concepts include gendered violence, institutional overlap, and survivor agency. The chapter concludes that institutions both shape and reinforce societal inequalities but also serve as sites of resistance where survivors organize to challenge gendered violence.


Ayesha Vemuri's article "Talk to Me: Towards a Politics of Transnational Feminist Solidarity" examines the challenges of fostering genuine transnational feminist solidarity in the context of global media representations of sexual violence. Vemuri critiques dominant media narratives that perpetuate neocolonial, classist, and casteist tropes, particularly in the aftermath of the 2012 Delhi gang rape incident. The Nirbhaya case refers to the gangrape and murder of a young woman, Jyoti Singh Pandey, on a moving bus in Delhi in December 2012.  Vemuri highlights how these narratives often fail to capture the complexities of gender-based violence in postcolonial societies, thereby hindering effective solidarity among feminists worldwide. As evidence, Vemuri discusses "Talk to Me," a public art intervention by the Bangalore-based feminist collective Blank Noise, which went viral on social media. This project exemplifies an alternative approach to addressing sexual violence by centering the voices and experiences of survivors, challenging prevailing stereotypes, and fostering nuanced conversations. Key concepts in the article include transnational feminist solidarity, neocolonial media representations, and the role of art in activism. Vemuri advocates for more inclusive and context-sensitive feminist practices that transcend simplistic portrayals and engage deeply with the lived realities of marginalized communities. “While the media brought deserved attention to issues of gendered violence, it neglected to discuss rape in private spaces, or investigate the sociocultural roots of sexual violence (Lodhia 2015; Mani 2014; Shandilya 2015). Instead, news was often sensationalist, provoking hysteria and stressing the dangers of public spaces for women (Bhattacharyya 2015; Chaudhuri 2015; Roychowdhury 2013).”
