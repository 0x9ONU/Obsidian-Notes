# Hertz Personal Essay Outline (Music Theme)

Built from two previous winning personal essays. Check the current Hertz prompt and length limit before drafting.

## What the two winners share

1. One unifying idea (science as narrative-building; stories) instead of a list of accomplishments.
2. Mostly about the person, not a research pitch.
3. Small, specific, concrete details carry the personality.
4. Structurally plain (chronological or topical), relying on content and clarity.
5. Both link the non-research material back to research.
6. Both are honest about difficulty or tradeoffs.

## Core idea

Bari sax, bass clarinet, and tuba are low-register, foundational voices. You're the part nobody notices when it works and everybody notices when it fails. That mirrors hardware: the layer everything else depends on, trusted without being verified. The essay's job is to show how playing these instruments across jazz, orchestra, and marching band shaped how you think about dependable foundations, and why that leads to hardware security.

## How to beat the blank page

- Write the section you find easiest first. The order you draft in doesn't have to be the order of the essay.
- Write a bad first sentence on purpose, then fix it after the section exists.
- Start with a scene or a fact, never with a claim about yourself. Facts are easy to write; claims are where people stall.
- Use the fill-in-the-blank starters below, then delete the scaffolding.

## Outline, with ways to start each section

### 1. Opening: the core idea through an image (about 10%)

Goal: the reader sees the foundation idea before you name it.

Starter options:

- **Start with a sound.** "The best thing a tuba can do is go unnoticed." Then explain.
- **Start with a missing part.** Describe what a piece sounds like when the bass line drops out. Then tell the reader that was you.
- **Start with a count-in.** Open on the beat before an entrance: where you're standing or sitting, what you're about to play, what's riding on it.
- **Start with a contrast.** "In jazz I [did X]. In orchestra I [did Y]. In marching band I [did Z]. The note was the same; the job was different."

Avoid: "I have always loved music" or "Music has been a big part of my life."

### 2. Origin story: one specific moment (about 15%)

Goal: a scene that shows curiosity or stubbornness, not a summary of your career.

Starter options:

- **Start at the first time.** "The first time I picked up a [instrument], I [specific thing that went wrong or surprised you]."
- **Start with a decision.** "I switched to [instrument] because [real reason]." The real reason is usually more interesting than the polished one.
- **Start with an object or place.** The case, the practice room, a rented instrument, the rehearsal hall. Describe it in two sentences, then say what happened there.
- **Start in the middle.** Drop into a rehearsal already in progress, then back up one sentence to explain how you got there.

Prompt to answer: what was the first instrument change that scared you, and why?

### 3. Development: music as the main thread (about 40%)

**Music (about 25%)** Goal: show different skills in the three settings without listing them.

Starter options for each setting:

- **Jazz.** Start with a moment you had to hold the harmonic floor under someone else's solo. "While the soloist [did X], my job was to [Y]."
- **Orchestra.** Start with the conductor. "A conductor can change [something] with one gesture, and the section has to [respond] together."
- **Marching band.** Start with the physical problem. "Playing [instrument] while moving means [specific difficulty]."
- **Transition between settings.** Start with the switch itself: the day you had to go from one ensemble to another and what you had to unlearn.

Prompt to answer: what did each setting demand that the others didn't?

**Supporting beats (about 15%, pick one or two)** Goal: add range and the professor angle without diluting the music.

Starter options:

- **Mentoring.** Start with the student's problem, not your role. "[Student] kept getting stuck on [X]."
- **Leadership.** Start with the decision. "As president I had to decide whether to [X]."
- **Community.** Start with the activity itself, in one concrete sentence. Keep only if it ties to the theme.

### 4. A moment of failure or difficulty (about 10%)

Goal: one honest setback, with what you changed.

Starter options:

- **Start with the mistake itself, plainly.** "I came in [early/late/wrong] on [piece]." No cushioning.
- **Start with the aftermath.** The silence, the look from the section leader, what you said or didn't say.
- **Start with the fix.** "The next week I [specific change]," then back up to explain what it fixed.

Prompt to answer: what did you change afterward, and did it work?

### 5. Bridge back to research (about 15%)

Goal: connect music to hardware security without forcing it.

Starter options:

- **Start with a parallel.** "A bass line and a hardware layer have something in common: [X]."
- **Start with a question.** "What happens when the foundation fails?" Then answer with the music failure and the hardware failure side by side.
- **Start with a decision.** "That's why I work on [bitstream reverse engineering]." Then give the music reason in one sentence.
- **Start with a consequence for how you work.** "Playing [instrument] taught me [specific habit], which is how I approach [research habit]."

Keep the technical pitch to a sentence or two. The bridge is the part most at risk in a music-heavy essay, so make it explicit.

### 6. Closing: forward-looking and understated (about 10%)

Goal: a plain, confident last line. Essay 1 ended "I will keep writing."

Starter options:

- **Return to the opening image.** Reuse the sound, count-in, or missing part from section 1, now with new meaning.
- **State what you'll keep doing.** "I will keep playing the parts nobody notices."
- **End on the next entrance.** A line about what's coming next, without grand claims.

Avoid: a closing line about perseverance, passion, or making the world safer.

## Style rules

- One organizing idea, stated early through an image.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions (named instruments, named pieces, named settings, numbers where honest).
- Plain, measured tone. Facts carry the credibility.
- Show one real failure.
- Don't stretch the metaphor until it snaps (essay 2's botany section).
- Don't end on a generic "perseverance and creativity" line.
- Don't turn the music section into a list of instruments and ensembles.

## Cautions

- Verify the current Hertz prompt and length limit; this outline is based on the winners' structure, not the official instructions.
- The proposed-field essay already covers the technical content. Keep overlap minimal so the personal essay adds something new.

## Open question

- Which single music moment is the most vivid? That is probably the opening or origin scene.
