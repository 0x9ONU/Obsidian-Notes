# Essay

The hall is dead silent for several seconds, just long enough to hear the air rustling against the programs in the audience. The conductor slowly raises his baton as the whole orchestra comes to attention. One, two, three, four: the conductor whips his hand and the auditorium suddenly gives way to the violins’ first phrase. The high brass join, bringing in the bright melody as the woodwinds balance it from underneath. All the other textures and colors come from around the symphony to form a beautiful piece. However, underneath all of the motion and brightness, a low and steady tone bellow on, without notice by the audience. Behind the strings, reeds, and double reeds sits the row of low instruments.  At the end of that row, I sit with my bass clarinet propped up against on the floor as I count my measures of rest to come in. On cue, a singular low note resonates out of the bell of the instrument, held ever so carefully under the melody. The audience would remember the trumpets articulating their precise melody or the sway of violins at the beginning of the piece. Unbeknownst to them, the piece would have fallen apart if it were not for the low winds and their dedication to their craft.

In 5th grade, my first instrument was the French horn, which I could not even make a proper sound on. After a week, my teacher recommended I try a different instrument, and I never looked back. In high school, I had to learn another instrument to be part of the jazz ensemble, which is where I learned baritone sax. After the next marching season, I heard a spot needed to be filled in the sousaphone section, which I took without hesitation. Then another spot opened up in our concert band, which needed a bass clarinet to fill the void left by a graduating senior. At Ohio Northern University, I joined the college versions of the same ensembles: bari sax for jazz, tuba for marching, and bass clarinet for orchestra. I would continue to play in all of these ensembles throughout my four years at Ohio Northern except for marching band, which I had to drop early on due to scheduling conflicts. 

Jazz band and orchestra rehearsed in the same room, but I filled two contrasting roles. In jazz, the notes written on the page were only the beginning. A trumpet could suddenly jump out and play its own melody, while a trombone could take over and play off of them. My job was to keep the ground steady under them. I held the chord changes, kept the time, and followed where the soloist was heading. Once, a tenor saxophone cut the solo too short and left a few bars open before the next soloist. I kept the changes moving and added a short phrase to lead the next player in without the audience knowing there was a mistake.

In orchestra, the page told the whole story. Every note, entrance, and dynamic was decided before the piece was rehearsed. The challenge came in providing a backbone for the instruments in front of me. When a low instrument plays its note, it generates both the fundamental frequency and a series of harmonics, which can help fill out the sound that higher pitched instruments make. Therefore, every low instrument must play in tune and with a strong tone to ensure the orchestra’s blend. Jazz needed me to adapt for improvisation, while orchestra asked me to follow a plan for consistency. Both ensembles, however, asked me to be the part you could count on.

Marching band was the one ensemble I had to leave earlier than I wanted to. During my sophomore year, the rehearsal time ended up overlapping with my study time and IEEE’s general meeting. I could not be in two places at once. I went with IEEE, and the low brass line went on without me. I missed the roar of the Saturday games, the many amazing people who made up the section, and all the fun traditions the tubas carried on. It was the first time I had to say no to a musical role that needed me. Being dependable also means knowing how much I can promise.

That role followed me outside the rehearsal halls. Over the summer, I advised an undergraduate on research I had worked on before. He got stuck on how to implement an automatic repeat request (ARQ) protocol on the Arduino testbeds, and the work could not move forward until the testbeds were fully functional. Instead of taking it over, I asked him questions about the problems he was having and provided my previous code and other approaches found by the literature. He did the primary research work, while I supported him in the background. By August, I was explaining less and listening more, and the work had become a paper.

Hardware is the low instrument in modern infrastructure. Like a catchy melody played by a violin or trumpet, most users only care about application-level features and performance. However, a single hardware flaw, like an out-of-tune note, could throw off everything built above it. I want to be the person who makes that foundation dependable. My research on FPGA bitstream reverse engineering aims to let people verify what is actually running on a chip, so the software above it can be trusted.

When the last chord fades, the applause will go to the violins, and I have come to love that. I would rather be the note that makes the melody possible.
